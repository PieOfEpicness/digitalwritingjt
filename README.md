Readings
storyland - nanette wylde
<p>A randomly generated short story, this deceptively simple piece of digital work definitely allows for a lot of absurdity and laughter depending on what’s generated. It starts each time with the Storyland letters flashing at the top and the sound of circus music, before the text slowly appears below. I think it’s pretty cool, and it allows for some laughs when you read it aloud for friends to hear.</p>

ah - k michel and dirk vis
<p>This work can be difficult to read at first, as the letters flow from one side of the screen to the other, bumping into and overlapping each other. But once you get into the rhythm of it, it’s like as the authors state, it feels like breathing, however visually. It’s a short and interesting piece, though there isn’t much to get out of it after a few minutes.</p>

ask me for the moon - john david zuern
<p>A digital poem set against an animated background, this is an experimental work dedicated to the more than 30,000 of local Hawai’ians who work for the hotels on the island, both documented and undocumented labour. It’s both moving and informative, and makes me wish for a better future for the local peoples.</p>

a modern ghost - artemio morales, et al
<p>A unique piece, this involves scrolling through a short story on a mobile device. Artwork and the story interconnect, as the protagonist reminisces on an adventure he had with his friends, and one young woman in particular. Scrolling through the piece and seeing the artwork change was great, and even though it was very short, it’s pretty memorable.</p>

decolonize e-literature? on weeding the e-lit garden - kathy inman berens
<p>This piece was about recognising what e-literature is, as well as how to recognise our own biases in that recognition. Does instagram poetry count? Do memes count? Are we eliminating potential authors of e-literature based on their differences, like other forms of creativity have done in the past? Classical music is often taught in the Western world as the only form of music theory that really counts, but there are so many different forms of music theory all around the world, but the white world won’t consider them because they’re created by people of colour. Other times is because someone is from a different social class, or from a whole other minority.</p>

electronic literature: what is it? - n. katherine hayles
<p>This piece helped me to define what e-literature is, where it started and its early pioneers, and where it may go in the future. I also really came to appreciate the writers that came before me, how their work has shaped the e-literature space today, and how I can contribute to that shaping myself. Literature has evolved so much over time, and I really look forward to seeing how it will continue to evolve in my lifetime.
</p>

## Week two readings

digital poesis impulse - jenny hedley
<p>This piece showed the step by step process to creating a piece of digital literature, from inception and inspiration, to the frustrations of Chat GPT, to the triumph of figuring out the right way to get code to work. Even though I’ve yet to see my own work come to fruition, I feel every part of Jenny’s journey to her own victory and publication, and look forward to my own end product. kissing the steak: the poetry of text generators - christopher t. funkhouser 
<p>This essay explores how text generators can work in the context of poetry. It feels like a collaboration between human and machine - we provide the words, the machine gives them order. This is how the Storyland example words, this is how our Dadaist poems work. I must admit, I’m not a poet, I don’t know how to appreciate poetry beyond “that sounded nice”, or in the case of the randomly generated poems, “that sounded pretty funny.” I’m not sure how much meaning there is to get from a text generated poem, aside from the interactivity of it.</p>

Readings

taroko gorge - nick monfort
<p>I found Taroko Gorge to be a fascinating poem. When I first read it, I wasn’t sure what was happening, but found it entrancing all the same. The way it flows like a river, different elements repeating, coming and going, it was mesmerising to watch as it flowed. This poem absolutely could not work in traditional print, being algorithmically designed and the fact that it moves as lines are generated. That’s what makes this particular piece of work different from print literature, or rather an evolution of it.</p>

the gathering cloud - j r carpenter
<p>The Gathering Cloud is a piece that I found to be deeply introspective, making me think about how much data I’m really using in my writing. That all has to be stored somewhere, it’s not in some metaphysical location, it exists physically in a database that uses a tremendous amount of power and water to sustain itself. The closest analogy to how this piece could potentially work in traditional print media would be a pop-up book. There’s plenty of words within the paragraphs that can be hovered over to give more information and context. The animations that fade in over each page are beautiful accompaniments to the writing, matching in metaphorical tone. Overall, I really loved this piece and the way it got me thinking about how my writing has a physical impact on the planet.</p>

first draft of the revolution - emily short and liza daly
<p>This was my personal favourite out of all the pieces. This work is closely related to choose-your-own-adventure books, being able to choose your own outcome to the story. I really enjoyed all the subtle variations to pick from, each leading to different reactions from NPC characters, and, of course, different endings. I think it’d take a lot more paper to print this than a traditional choose-your-own-adventure book, so digital is the place for it to be. I loved being able to take part in how the story was told, and felt like my choices had consequences. I think that’s a sign of how well this piece was written.</p>

shade - andrew plotkin
<p>Shade is not something I got very far in. As much as I have an exploratory nature in all the games I play, that doesn’t really translate well to the old fashioned text adventure. It’s similar in nature again to a choose-your-own-adventure book, though far more gamified and allowing for player agency, if one knows how to play a text adventure. Still, it wouldn’t work in traditional print, due to requiring player input to progress, needing more than just the turning to a particular page.</p>


oulipo, introduction - warren f. mott jr
<p>This introductory chapter was something I struggled to have any thoughts on. I wasn’t sure what I was listening to (I listened through this half a dozen times) and still have trouble understanding what this is leading to without the context of the rest of the text. I think it’s going over several examples from a writing group called the Oulipo?</p>

readings

response to ergodic literature, hypertext aesthetics - espen j aarseth
<p>This work explores the beginnings of hypertexts, its uses, and its early pioneers. It’s interesting to see what is essentially the collective origins of all us as digital writers. We wouldn’t be doing this course if it weren’t for the writers that came before us. It was definitely interesting to read the debate about who is considered the true author if the reader is also an active participant in the writing. I also found it interesting to realise just how difficult it would be to correct something that would be printed, versus something digital. Like, of course that would be the case, but you don’t really think of it. It’s a little easier to edit a film or series after release (I’m reminded of 13 Reasons Why on Netflix, though you can only take something out rather than add something in), but it’s much easier and quicker when it’s digital writing, where it’s literally at your fingertips.</p>

<p>It was interesting to read how the internet was discussed at the time that it was written. We’ve come so far with technology in such a short amount of time (the AI singularity is upon us!), and it’s interesting to think about where we’ll be in the future. We don’t really think of where the internet came from now, and what we used to be without it.</p>

<p>Ergodic literature is something that I’ve definitely come across before, even if I didn’t have the language to say what it was. Many times I’ve read or watched something that felt just like random events stuck together, without a narrative flow connecting them. I usually associate these types of stories with negative views, as in I don’t find them to be very well written and usually a waste of time. However, considering the broad definition of it that Aarseth puts forth, there’s more things that can be put in this category that I do consider (in my opinion) to be good. Episodic TV shows, books and films can fall under this umbrella, such as The Simpsons, Sherlock Holmes and the Knives Out film series.</p>

<p>There is a fair amount in this reading dedicated to a text called afternoon, but it talks about how it was written rather than about the story itself. Considering its place as one of the first pieces of electronic literature written and published, I understand its relevance to the discussion that Aarseth puts forth, as well as his argument for this work to be considered ergodic, despite not having read it, only the wikipedia page for it.</p>

mapping ideas
<p>When it came to mapping out my piece, it wasn’t at all linear to me. I started out knowing I wanted to write something set within the Remedy Gaming Universe, and then I brainstormed from there. I remembered a little piece of interesting lore from the game, about how sometimes office workers just go missing when the Oldest House shifts, and that’s what sparked the rest of it. I took a character that I had created and briefly wrote about in a fanfiction, fleshed her out, and plopped her down. I knew she had to go through a series of trials, which I found difficult to write as prose, as I was thinking about it from a gaming perspective. There’s also some visuals that I can allude to but can’t really show, I have no artistic skills to give them justice, but I worked around what I could, and hope to leave the rest to the reader’s imagination.</p>

## Week seven readings

the paradox of poetic gaming - astrid ensslin
<p>This piece explores the nature of poetic games, what counts as one, as well as how they work and the type of people who play them. There’s also some much warranted criticism directed at the gaming industry, as well as some of the playerbase. Though I’m not sure how true the statement regarding violent video games being used by governments for military training purposes is, that sounds very conspiracy theory-ish. There were some passages that I found had a condescending tone regarding popular/mainstream games and who plays them, and that art games were of a higher caliber just because they could be considered art.</p>

poetic playlands - jason nelson
<p>This piece details one man’s journey into a unique gaming landscape. Jason has built many games over the years, but only a handful have been successful, as in played by many people. It was an interesting read seeing what went into the process of making a successful indie poetry game, as well as the aftermath of it crashing onto the scene. I felt some familiarity with him trying to make a follow up to something people liked and failing for that to get any traction.</p>
